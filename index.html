The cruelty of the U.S. visa system

I recently visited Mexico City. If you're a US citizen and you want to visit Mexico City from DC, you don't have to plan ahead. Famously, you don't even have to speak Spanish. You land at Benito Juarez International Airport, and a tired but friendly Aduana agent talks to you for 10 seconds before punching a green stamp into your passport, allowing you to visit for 30 days. You proceed to the city's expansive Metro system (or subject yourself to LA-grade traffic on surface streets).


American citizens rarely have to wait long to visit another country.


If you're a Mexican citizen and you want to visit the US, you first have to fill out a DS-160 form. The State Department says this should take roughly 90 minutes. Questions include things like "Have you ever been 10-printed?" and "Have you ever served in, been a member of, or been involved with a paramilitary unit, vigilante unit, rebel group, guerrilla group, or insurgent organizations?" (If yes, please explain). You must answer the questions in English. It's unclear if the 90-minute estimate includes the time it takes to learn English. Then you have to schedule an in-person interview with a consular officer. The wait time for an appointment is six months. That officer will ask you questions about your personal finances, your family ties to Mexico, and your job. Assuming you "pass" the interview (whether you pass is largely up to this consular officer) you'll have to pay $185 for the privilege of visiting the US.


The impacts of these wait times on individual people is brutal.


Roberto was a U.S. Citizen who grew up in Colombia. He died well before his time. Roberto was beloved by his friends and family. He spent his entire professional life helping people in need. Roberto's brother still lives in Colombia, and tried to fly to the U.S. to attend Roberto's funeral. The U.S. Department of State refused to issue him a visa. He could not attend Roberto's funeral.


Our visa system was unequal, even before the Trump administration made that Family Guy bit with the "skin color chart" our country's explicit foreign policy.


There are, for example, four U.S. missions in Australia, but there is only one in Bangladesh. Australia has a population of 27 million. Bangladesh has a population of 175 million. There is essentially no visa wait time for Australian citizens. The wait time in Bangladesh is 358 days.

Residents of equatorial countries, like Colombia, are punished particularly hard by America's visa system. The World Cup has provided several examples of this.

The mother of Cape Verde goalie Vozinha missed her son's game-saving performance because of the exhorbitant visa costs the U.S. imposes on Cape Verdeans, until Marco Rubio intervened to waive a $15,000 bond requirement.

Omar Artan, a FIFA referee from Somalia, has overseen world-class matches for a decade, including during the African Cup of Nations. He was supposed to referee this World Cup, which he said was "the biggest dream of my life." All of his documents were in order and he had a visa. U.S. immigration police detained Omar in Miami International Airport and questioned him for 11 hours. They accused him of being a terrorist, an absurd, insulting claim for which they have provided no evidence, because there is none. Then they forced him to fly to Somalia. This is a man who will referee the UEFA Super Cup later this year, who was named Africa's best male referee, and whom the President of Somalia personally praised for his integrity.

The entire Iranian national team had to relocate from the US to Tijuana after Customs denied entry to their support staff.
